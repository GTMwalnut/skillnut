# Pilot sizing, costs, and the budget conversation

Load this when the plan carries an investment section: any pilot proposal, any scope of work with an outbound track, or a user asking "how many inboxes do we need" or "what will this cost". It covers five things, in the order you use them: the revenue math (when the client gives a revenue goal), the work-back sizing, the three cost buckets, Option A next to the pilot, and the answers to budget pushback.

Every number in the worked examples below is illustrative. The rules (sends per inbox, inboxes per domain, the warm-up share, sending days) are working defaults from real programs. Re-check provider prices on the day; they move.

## 0. The revenue math: from the revenue goal to the people to reach

Use this when the client states a revenue goal ("we want to cross a million in a year") instead of a meeting target. It produces the meeting target that section 1 starts from, plus the reach and the account count. The whole chain, in order:

**revenue goal > revenue per customer (deal size x how long a customer stays) > new customers a month > meetings > replies > people to reach (at two reply rates) > companies (at a few contacts each) > who those companies are (bands and partners) > domains and inboxes (section 1) > cost (section 2) > commercial model**

| Step | Question | How to work it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What is the goal, and by when? | Revenue in a time frame, with the stretch figure noted separately |
| 2 | What does one customer bring in? | Average deal size times the months (or years) a customer stays. Where there are several packages, use a blended average and say so |
| 3 | How many new customers a month? | Goal divided by revenue per customer, spread over the months of the time frame |
| 4 | How many meetings a month? | Customers times the meeting-to-customer ratio. Working assumption when there is no history: 1 in 5 meetings becomes a customer |
| 5 | How many replies? | Meetings times the reply-to-meeting ratio. Working assumption: about half of replies turn into a meeting |
| 6 | How many people to reach a month? | Replies divided by the reply rate, **shown at two rates side by side** (a cautious one and a hopeful one, for example 0.5% and 1%), email and LinkedIn combined |
| 7 | How many companies? | People divided by the contacts per company (3 is the working default) |

Every ratio in steps 4 to 6 is an assumption until the client's own numbers replace it; the plan says so in one line under the table.

**Worked example (illustrative).** Goal: 1.2 million in new annual revenue within a year; average deal about 2,000 a month, customers stay about 10 months, so about 20,000 per customer.

| Step | At 0.5% reply rate | At 1% reply rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| New customers needed a month | 5 | 5 |
| Meetings (1 in 5 becomes a customer) | 25 | 25 |
| Replies (about half become a meeting) | 50 | 50 |
| People to reach a month | 10,000 | 5,000 |
| Companies (3 people each) | about 3,300 | about 1,700 |

**Aim above the math, and say why.** Plan reach above what the table needs: reply and conversion rates can come in lower than assumed, and meetings booked in one month mostly become customers one or two sales cycles later, so pipeline must be built ahead. Ask for the client's usual time from first meeting to signed deal and use it to set how far ahead.

**Choose the motion by deal size before sizing infrastructure.**

| Deal size | Motion | What it means for the plan |
|---|---|---|
| Large annual contracts (five figures a year and up) | Signal-led | Fewer accounts, a buying group per account, signals worth paying for, slower and deeper sequences |
| Small subscriptions (tens to a few hundred a month) | Volume | A wide TAM; short, plain messages that ask for a meeting or a sign-up; signals only where they are cheap and easy to spot (tracking them at scale costs more than simply asking the right person); the TAM rotated, meaning touched with a couple of messages, cleaned of bounces and replies, then touched again, never sooner than the 100-day cooldown |

At small deal sizes, do the break-even out loud too: the tools plus a GTM engineer's time only pay back at high volume. If they cannot, say so and point at the founder readiness check.

**Who fills the reach.** Split the companies by revenue band and say who is contacted in each, because the right title changes with company size (for example: the founder or CEO at the smallest band, the head of the function and their team in the middle, and the managers and directors below the C-level at the largest band, holding the C-level back for now). Start with every band, then shift effort to the bands that answer. If partners (agencies, consultants, service firms who serve the same buyers) can resell or refer, run them as a second group with their own message and a revenue-share offer. List the never-fit company types next to the bands.

**Then hand over to section 1:** the people-to-reach figure becomes the contacts a month, emails a month follow from the sequence length (follow-ups included), and inboxes and domains follow from the inbox rule. Cost goes into the three buckets (section 2), and the commercial model closes the chain (see the retainer variants in the main skill's investment section).

## 1. Work-back sizing: from the target to the domains

Start from what the client wants to buy (SQLs or meetings a month), never from a send volume. Volume is the answer, not the input.

| Step | Question | Default to use, then replace with the client's own numbers |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | How many SQLs or meetings a month, at the start and at steady state? | Ask for both. A real target always ramps: a few in the first months, more by month four to six |
| 2 | How many positive replies does that need? | SQLs divided by the share of positive replies that become SQLs. If the client has no history, mark the share "assumed" |
| 3 | How many contacts reached a month? | Positive replies divided by the positive reply rate per contact. The plan's measurement table carries the starting ranges. Where the rate is a guess, show the result at a cautious and a hopeful rate side by side, as in section 0, and size the infrastructure on the cautious one |
| 4 | How many emails a month? | Contacts times emails per contact in the monthly cycle (usually 2 to 3) |
| 5 | How many inboxes? | Emails a month divided by about 264 (see the rule below) |
| 6 | How many domains? | Inboxes divided by 3 |
| 7 | Does the universe hold it? | Contacts a month times the months of the program, against the real TAM, after the cooldown rule |

**The inbox rule, and where 264 comes from.** A Google inbox sends about 15 cold emails a day. Microsoft and SMTP inboxes send about 5, so a Microsoft-heavy pool needs roughly three times the inboxes. Keep 3 inboxes per domain. Run an 80/20 split: 80 percent of inboxes send while 20 percent sit in warm-up as spares, rotated weekly, so a burnt inbox is swapped without stopping the campaign. Count about 22 sending days a month. So one inbox in the pool carries 15 x 22 x 0.8 = about 264 emails a month.

**Worked example (illustrative).** A client wants 8 SQLs a month at steady state.

| Step | Maths | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Positive replies needed | 8 SQLs at an assumed 40% positive-to-SQL | 20 positive replies |
| Contacts a month | 20 at an assumed 1% positive reply rate per contact | 2,000 contacts |
| Emails a month | 2,000 x 3 emails per contact | 6,000 emails |
| Inboxes | 6,000 / 264 | 23 inboxes (about 18 sending, 5 warming) |
| Domains | 23 / 3 | 8 secondary domains |
| Universe check | 2,000 a month x 6 months | 12,000 contacts needed; if the real TAM is 6,000, the target or the channel mix must change |

Say the universe check out loud in the plan. With the catalogue's own rule (a contact who did not answer waits 100 days before the next touch, and anyone who replied is never cold-emailed again), a small TAM runs dry fast. When it does, the honest fixes are: lower the target, add LinkedIn and warmer sources, or widen the ICP with the client's agreement. Never quietly raise the send volume to hit the target.

**Rules that ride with the sizing:**
- Secondary domains only, never the primary domain and never subdomains of it. Variations of the brand name on .com are the safe default.
- New inboxes warm for at least 14 days before the first real send; 21 to 30 days is better. If the client already owns warmed secondary inboxes, the setup shortens.
- Size for the ramp, not just the peak. Month one usually runs a smaller pool, so the inbox bill grows with the volume.
- A domain-protection tool (for example EmailGuard) earns its place at high domain counts; at pilot scale, watch for two or three months first, then decide.

## 2. The three cost buckets

Put every cost in one of three buckets, and show them separately. A single blended number invites a single blended objection.

| Bucket | What is in it | How it is billed | Illustrative month (the example above) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Infrastructure | Secondary domains (one-time, roughly $12 each) and inboxes (roughly $3 to $4 each a month from resellers such as Scaledmail, Zapmail or Inboxkit) | Pass-through at actual cost | 23 inboxes, about $85 a month; 8 domains, about $100 once |
| B. Outbound tools | Data (a contact database or pay-as-you-go enrichment), the sequencer (Email Bison, Instantly, Smartlead), LinkedIn automation (HeyReach, per sender seat), and any intent tool | Pass-through at actual cost, or the client holds the subscription | Sequencer, one or two LinkedIn seats, data credits |
| C. Retainer | The work: strategy, list building, copy, running the infrastructure, reply handling, reporting | The agency's fee, with the meetings included and any per-meeting fee above it | Quoted on its own line |

How to present it:
- **Buckets A and B are at cost.** Say so, and show the line items. If a partner (for example an intent-data vendor) runs part of the scope, split bucket C into each party's scope so the client sees who does what.
- **Anything tied to the client's identity sits in the client's name** (their CRM, their ad accounts, their WhatsApp number); commodity infrastructure can sit on the agency's plan. This matches the investment rule in the main skill.
- **Show cost per SQL at base and at stretch:** total of A + B + C divided by the SQL target. Let the client compare it with what their other channels cost them. Do not quote an industry benchmark you cannot source.

## 3. Option A next to the pilot

When the client arrives with a big volume goal, show two options side by side and recommend the smaller one. The comparison does the persuading; the recommendation just names it.

| | Option A: scale now | Option B: 90-day pilot (recommended) |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | The client's full goal from month one (illustrative: 100,000+ emails a month) | About a fifth or less of that (illustrative: 20,000 emails a month) |
| Infrastructure | Sized for the peak: many domains and inboxes bought up front | Sized for the pilot, with room to add domains in month three |
| Segments | Every segment at once | Warm lists first, then one or two cold segments with signals |
| What it risks | Burns a large part of the TAM before anyone knows which message works | Learns what works on a slice of the TAM, then puts volume behind it |
| Cost | Highest bucket A and B from day one | Small A and B; the same quality of work in C |
| When it fits | Messaging and segments are already proven on this audience | Anything new: new audience, new offer, or no outbound history |

The pilot's shape, carried into the timeline section:

| When | What happens |
|---|---|
| Days 1 to 14 | Setup: domains and inboxes bought and warming, lists built, copy approved. LinkedIn can start in week one; it needs no warm-up |
| Month 1 | Warm lists (CRM reactivation, engaged contacts) and the first slice of the cold list |
| Month 2 | Cold outreach on signals, at pilot volume |
| Month 3 | Volume behind what worked, the rest retired; the readout and the decision |

Name the KPIs and the checkpoints in the proposal. Agree the success bar for the readout before the pilot starts, and give the client a clean exit if it is missed. A pilot whose success bar is written after the results is not a pilot.

## 4. Budget objections: what to say

Most budget pushback is one of six lines. Answer the real worry under it, not the sentence.

| They say | What is usually under it | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| "This is more than we expected" | One big number with no breakdown | Split it into the three buckets. Show that A and B are at cost and only C is the fee. Then offer the real levers: the client buys the domains themselves, start with one LinkedIn seat instead of two (tools are month-to-month), and let the inbox pool ramp with volume |
| "Can we start smaller?" | They want proof before commitment | Yes: that is the pilot. Price it as its own thing, separate from the retainer that follows, and name the normal retainer first so the pilot reads as the smaller step it is. Ask for the pilot fee up front; half up front is a fair fallback |
| "Can you work on results only?" | They want you to carry the risk | Offer the shared-risk structure instead: a retainer with meetings included plus a per-meeting fee above it. Pure pay-per-meeting means the agency funds the client's tools and the client's learning, and the plan says why that does not work |
| "We can only fund a few hundred a month in total" | The budget cannot cover the tools | Say it plainly: email outbound needs a real floor for tools, data and infrastructure (in recent programs, several hundred to about a thousand dollars a month before any fee). A client who will not fund the tools is a red flag, not a discount request. Offer the founder-led and warm-source path instead (see `founder-readiness-check.md`) |
| "Why 90 days? Can we do 30?" | Fear of lock-in | The first two to three weeks are setup and warm-up, so 30 days buys almost no live sending. KPIs mean something by month three or four. Offer the exit at the pre-agreed checkpoint instead of a shorter term |
| "Our price point is low, will this even pay back?" | Unit economics | Do the break-even in front of them: monthly cost of the program divided by what one new customer is worth. If the product sells for a few hundred dollars a year, email outbound rarely pays back; it fits products priced in the thousands, or a lifetime value well into five figures. Say so, and point at the cheaper channels first |

The principle under all six: a budget objection answered with a discount teaches the client that the first price was padded. Answer it with structure (buckets, phases, who holds which subscription) and keep the fee.
