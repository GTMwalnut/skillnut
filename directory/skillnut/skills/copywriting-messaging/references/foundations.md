# Foundations module (loaded on demand)

Foundations is the messaging base that every other module stands on. Run it before Research or Write when the user cannot say in two plain sentences what they sell, who it is for, and what the buyer does today instead. Three pieces, run any or all:

| Piece | What it answers | Who reads it |
|---|---|---|
| A. Positioning canvas + statement | Why would the right buyer pick this over what they do today? | The founder, the copywriter, the website |
| B. Plain-English product briefing | What is this, in words a new SDR could repeat on a call? | Anyone writing or sending outreach |
| C. Named-account value brief | What is this worth to one specific big target, in their own cost terms? | The seller, a proposal, a deck |

Inputs, best first: sales-call and discovery-call transcripts (the buyer's own words), the website, product docs and any public code repository, case studies, an existing deck. Calls beat everything else, because the buyer's words are the words the copy should use.

The evidence rules from the main file apply here too: a claim with no source is labeled, never presented as fact.

## A. The positioning canvas (five parts, in this order)

Work through the parts in order. Each one constrains the next: you cannot name your unique attributes until you know what you are being compared with.

| Part | The question | How to fill it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Competitive alternatives | If this product did not exist, what would the buyer do? | List 2 to 4 approaches, each as a short group: buy a purpose-built tool in the space, build it in-house on a general platform, stay with the old tool or process, hand it to people or an agency, or do nothing. "Do nothing" is almost always on the list. An alternative can be a state the buyer lives with ("no way to see which jobs are running late until the customer complains") rather than a named competitor |
| 2. Unique attributes | What can this do that the alternatives cannot? | Specific and countable ("checks every job against a set of trade-specific quality steps, not adapted from a generic project tool"). For each attribute, add a one-line "compare with": what the closest alternative does instead. If a capability is not shipped yet, mark it "upcoming" |
| 3. Value | What do those attributes make possible for the buyer? | Group into two or three value themes. Each theme gets one or two supporting attributes from part 2. Name what is leaking or lost today, in the buyer's terms, not features |
| 4. Target customer | Who cares most about that value? | Account traits: industry, size, setup, the situation that makes the pain acute ("several branches, standards uneven across them"). If there are too few customers to see a pattern yet, say so plainly ("too soon to lock; we need a pattern across 8 to 10 accounts") and give the working guess |
| 5. Market category | What frame helps the buyer understand it fast? | A short phrase a buyer would say out loud. Give a near-term category buyers already search for, and, only if relevant, a longer-term option labeled as an option |

Then three short outputs built from the canvas:

1. **Positioning statement, one paragraph.** The shape that works: what they already know is costing them, then what they cannot see, then what you are (the category), then what you do, then why it is different. Example (invented): "You already know a late job costs you the renewal. What you can't see is everything else costing you the same way. Bellwether is job-quality tracking for field service teams: it flags the late jobs, checks every visit against the steps that matter, and helps the team fix it before it costs the account."
2. **ICP summary, one paragraph.** The account type, the size band, and the situation that makes them best fit.
3. **The 30-second "what do you do?" talk track.** Spoken, plain, ending on the bottom line for the buyer.

Rules:
- **New terms the team wants to evangelize stay out of cold copy** until the buyer already uses them. Put them in a "terms to evangelize" box; the cold copy uses the buyer's existing words.
- **Ideas that are not decided go in a "parked" box,** not in the statement.
- The canvas feeds the rest of this skill: the competitive alternatives are the "messy process it replaces" that Research hunts for; the value themes are first drafts of [PAIN] and [MECHANISM] in Write.

## B. The plain-English product briefing

For technical products (infrastructure, AI, security, developer tools), the person writing or sending outreach often cannot explain the product. This briefing fixes that. A reader with no coding background should finish it able to explain the product on a call.

The language rule comes from real campaigns: founders and executives now say upfront that they mark lofty, AI-sounding emails as spam. "Balancing company vision with market execution" lost to "co-founders who also run marketing". Write at explain-it-to-a-friend level, and when you must use a term, explain it in the same sentence.

Sections, in this order:

1. **The setup: what problem even exists.** Start with the change in the world, then the problem it created, with no product name yet. One short story of how the problem happens day to day ("someone on the team sets up their own workaround to get the job done, and nobody in IT knows it exists").
2. **One main analogy, before and after.** Pick one picture (a badge system for a building, a power strip, an air-traffic control tower) and map every part of the product to a part of the picture. One analogy carried all the way beats five half-used ones. If the problem is a new version of an old one, say so ("the same idea as shadow IT, a decade on").
3. **What it actually is, in one sentence.**
4. **The client's own short version.** If the founder or client gave a clean one-line framing on a call, quote it and mark it client-sanctioned. Also write down what they did not say: an open question (for example, which angle to open a cold email with) is a decision for the owner, not for the briefing.
5. **What is finished and what is coming (the honest maturity ladder).** Four rungs: in production, late beta, just launching, internal only. Never pitch a rung higher than it is. Many founders say it themselves, in words like "I don't want to sell past where we are"; match that candour. Internal-only work becomes a "want to help shape this as a design partner?" conversation, never a product claim.
6. **A second story, if one is emerging.** For example a cost story next to a risk story. Numbers that come from an internal document are flagged "verify with the client before using in writing".
7. **The two things that make it different,** each with "why this matters in a sales conversation" in one or two lines.
8. **Proof stories in plain English.** Before, what they did, after, with the number. Use only stories the client has cleared for naming; otherwise anonymize ("a large bank").
9. **How to use it in a conversation:**
   - The one-liner, if the reader remembers only one thing.
   - Signals a prospect has the problem (things to listen for on calls or spot in job posts), with the strongest fit signal named, and what makes the pitch land weaker.
   - Credible facts from real calls (setup time, how a trial works, who joins calls).
   - Honest caveats: where there is no reference customer yet, say so and do not promise one.
10. **A low-friction first offer.** Something free and fast that shows the problem in their own environment ("a quick free check that shows what is already running in their own setup, including the parts nobody signed off"). It becomes the offer door in Write.
11. **Glossary.** Every term the reader might meet, with a plain-English meaning, in a table.

Hand-off: the glossary and the one-liner go into the shared hygiene check (no term in a cold email that the glossary had to explain); the signals list goes into the Research signal map; the first offer goes into Write.

## C. The named-account value brief (one page per big target)

For a handful of large named targets (enterprise accounts, a proposal, a deck), a one-page brief that speaks in the target's own cost terms. Not for volume: five accounts, not five hundred.

Sections:

1. **Their cost model.** How the work your product touches is paid for in their world, with one worked line: "Firms like this typically spend about X percent of a project on [the phase]; on a [size] project that is roughly [amount], and about [share] of that is internal coordination that is hard to bill." Every percentage is either from the target's own public material, from an industry source you can cite, or labeled "client estimate" or "assumed".
2. **The core problems that grow with scale.** Five to seven bullets, each one a structural issue that multiplies across regions, offices or projects ("each region runs its own supplier follow-up", "supplier data is self-reported, nothing checks it").
3. **What you deliver, as measurable outcomes.** Three or four outcomes as ranges ("30 to 50 percent fewer follow-up hours"). Each range traces to real customer results; if it is a target rather than a result, the brief says "target".
4. **How it reduces the labour.** The list of steps the product automates, in plain words.
5. **What that is worth to them,** in their unit (per state, per region, per project), labeled as an estimate.
6. **The standardization angle,** for multi-site targets: one shared database, one process, one view.

Worked example (invented company, invented numbers): a brief for a national facilities-maintenance firm opens with "Firms like this spend roughly 3 percent of contract value on bid coordination; on a 40 million contract that is about 1.2 million, and close to a third of it is internal follow-up that is never billed." Then six scale problems, three outcome ranges each marked "from two comparable customers", and an estimate per region marked "assumed, confirm with their ops lead".

Hand-off: the cost line is the [FIGURE] for Write's Day 15 email and the opening of a proposal; the scale problems are the [PAIN] options; the outcomes are the [METRIC] candidates (only the ones from real results).
