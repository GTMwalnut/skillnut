- Play back their choice, what they sell, who they call, and their prep budget in one short sentence.
- **Match the tier to the prep budget, and say why:** no prep means tier one (one reason for calling that fits a whole segmented list); ten minutes means tier two (one insight true of the segment, not the person); two to three minutes means tier three (a real per-account signal, bridged to its consequence).

- If their "segment" is actually a broad, loose list, flag it kindly: a tier-one opener needs a list tight enough that one reason for calling genuinely fits everyone, and say plainly when the list, not the opener, is the real issue.

## The engine: the cold-call-opener method (for the AI only; never shown raw to the user)

### The spec: an opener buys twenty seconds

An opener has one job. It does not book the meeting (that is the whole call's job), it does not qualify (nobody answers qualifying questions from a stranger), and it does not pitch (cramming the pitch into the first breath is why they hang up). It buys the next twenty seconds, in which your reason for calling actually lands. Holding this spec is what stops a seller cramming the reason, the value proposition and the ask into one breath out of fear of being cut off. Prospects hang up on people who sound like they are racing a clock and stay on for people who sound unhurried.

The standard failure, to name for the user: opening with your own name and company, so the prospect spends your first ten seconds working out whether they should know you, and misses your reason.

### Delivery: the larger half (teach this alongside every script)

A mediocre opener said calmly beats a perfect one said anxiously. What good sounds like:

- **Slower than feels natural**, with real pauses, not filled ones. The pace that feels too slow to you sounds normal to them.
- **Downward inflection.** Upward inflection at the end of a statement turns it into a question, and questions sound like asking permission.
- **No apology**, in the words or the tone. Apologising for calling confirms you should not have called.
- **No performed enthusiasm.** Brightness that is not really there reads as a script, and a script is exactly what they are listening for.

The frame to give the seller: you are two adults working out whether there is a fit. You are not begging for time and you are not beneath them. Newer sellers calling senior titles need this most, and their real advantage is pattern recognition: the buyer sees one company, the seller sees what is happening across dozens of similar ones, which is a legitimate reason to be on the phone and should be audible.

**The two-week delivery fix:** record all your calls for a week and listen only for how you sound to a stranger; mark the exact moments you sound like a seller (there will be a pattern and it is almost always pace); find a phone manner you admire and copy how they hold silence; record again and compare; and ask someone who has never cold called whether you sound like a person. Two weeks is enough to hear the change.

### The nine scripts, in three tiers (ordered by preparation)

Every script is a template to adapt and deliver, never read robotically. Fill the bracketed fields. For each, know when it fits and when it fails, and say the fail condition to the user.

**Tier one, no preparation. Run at volume on any list tight enough that one reason for calling fits everyone.**

1. **The honest cold call (candour).**
   > "Hi [NAME], it's [YOUR NAME] from [COMPANY]. I'll be upfront, this is a cold call. Can I take thirty seconds to tell you why I'm calling?"
   Variants: "I know I've caught you out of the blue, is now a terrible time?" or "You weren't expecting my call, but can I steal thirty seconds?"
   Works because naming the call for what it is disarms someone braced for a seller pretending otherwise, and thirty seconds is small enough to grant. Fits sellers who can be candid without apologising, in markets where directness reads as respect. Fails when delivered nervously, or half-done: naming it and then sounding sorry.

2. **Direct, with a disarming question (fastest to the point).**
   > "Hey [NAME], it's [YOUR NAME] with [COMPANY]." (pause, let them respond) "I'm calling about [TOPIC]. If you don't mind me asking, do you still handle that, or am I completely lost?"
   Works because "or am I completely lost" assumes you might be wrong, which removes the pressure, and once they correct you they have asked you a question. Fits time-pressed prospects and transactional motions. Fails if you cannot name a specific topic; "I'm calling about sales" is not a topic and they hear that instantly.

3. **Problem first (never mentions you).**
   > "Hey [NAME], quick one and then I'll get out of your way. Most [JOB TITLE]s I speak to at [COMPANY SIZE] companies are dealing with [SPECIFIC PROBLEM] right now. Is that landing anywhere near you, or are you in better shape than most?"
   Works because it never mentions you until they engage, and the closing question is binary and slightly flattering. Fits lists so tight that one problem genuinely applies to everyone on them. Fails if the problem is generic: the test is whether they think "that is me" or "that is everyone". "Compliance is hard" gets a brush-off.

**Tier two, ten minutes per segment. Something true of the whole list, not the individual. The best return on time of the three tiers.**

4. **Permission-based, relevance first (curiosity gap).**
   > "Hey [NAME], I was just taking a look at [THEIR WEBSITE / PROFILE / JOB AD] and had a question for you. It's [YOUR NAME] over at [COMPANY]. Have you got a moment to help me out?"
   Works because mentioning a question without asking it creates a gap people stay on the line to close. Fits relationship-driven motions and conversational sellers. Fails when the thing you looked at is obviously generic; if it could be said to anyone in the segment, use opener 3 and stop pretending.

5. **The segment hypothesis (you carry information).**
   > "Hi [NAME], [YOUR NAME] here from [COMPANY]. I've spent the last month speaking to [JOB TITLE]s at [SEGMENT] companies, and there's a pattern I keep hearing that I wanted to check with you. Have you got sixty seconds?"
   Works because it positions you as carrying information rather than asking for something, and senior people are chronically curious what their peers are doing. Fits senior titles and isolated buyers. Fails if you have not actually done the calls: you must be able to say what the pattern is when they ask, and they will ask.

6. **Peer reference (proximity).**
   > "Hey [NAME], [YOUR NAME] from [COMPANY]. We've been doing some work with [COMPARABLE COMPANY] on [PROBLEM], and your name came up as someone in a similar spot. Fair to give you thirty seconds on why, or have I got the wrong end of it?"
   Works because proximity lowers the "who is this random person" reflex. Fits markets where you have real customers who genuinely resemble the prospect. Fails the moment you overstate it: "your name came up" must be true or defensibly true, or you have opened with a lie they can smell.

**Tier three, two to three minutes per account. What makes these work is not the research, it is the bridge from what you found to the problem it creates.**

7. **Signal led (bridge, do not stop).**
   > "Hi [NAME], [YOUR NAME] from [COMPANY]. I saw [OBSERVABLE SIGNAL] and it made me want to call rather than email. Teams doing that usually run into [CONSEQUENCE] about a quarter later. Have you hit that yet, or has it not surfaced?"
   Works because the bridge in line three is the whole thing: a signal alone is flattery, a signal joined to the problem it creates is relevance, and that bridge is the step automated tools consistently fail at. Fits any account with a genuine catalyst or symptom whose consequence you can name. Fails if you stop at the signal, and never use an intent signal (see guardrails).

8. **Post-touch callback (highest-percentage moment).**
   > "Hi [NAME], [YOUR NAME] from [COMPANY]. I sent you something on [DAY] about [TOPIC] and figured a call would be quicker than another email. Did it land, or shall I give you the ten-second version?"
   Works because it is not really a cold call, which is the point: it converts a sequence into a conversation, and a ten-second version is an easy yes. Fits mid-sequence, especially after a profile view or an opened email. Fails if you did not actually send anything; nothing survives them checking.

9. **Content mirror (their own words).**
   > "Hi [NAME], [YOUR NAME] from [COMPANY]. You wrote something recently about [THEIR STATED PRIORITY], and it lined up with something I keep running into. I'd rather ask you about it than pitch you anything. Two minutes?"
   Works because a published priority hands you their language and an internal agenda item stated out loud, and mirroring it is the closest a cold call gets to being invited. Fits prospects who post, and pairs well with a hard timing signal like active hiring. Fails if you did not read it properly; referencing a post you skimmed is worse than not referencing it at all.

### The test: nine openers are nine hypotheses, not an answer

None of the nine is the right one. The right one is whatever works for the user's voice, market and persona, and the only way to find it is a proper A/B test. Eight rules:

1. **Pick two, not nine.** One from tier one, one from tier two. Testing nine at once is unreadable noise.
2. **Isolate one variable.** Change the opener and nothing else: same list, same reason for calling, same time of day.
3. **Split evenly, alternate by call, not by day**, so day-of-week effects cancel.
4. **Get to volume:** a hundred connects per variant is a reasonable floor. Below it you are reading noise as signal.
5. **Run it two weeks**, so one bad Tuesday does not decide your opener for a year.
6. **Measure conversation rate:** still talking at thirty seconds. Not meetings booked.
7. **Declare a winner late**, only when the gap is clear and consistent.
8. **Keep the loser.** It may be right for a specific persona or seniority; log what you learned.

Why the metric matters more than the method: meetings booked measures everything downstream of the opener too, so a strong opener on a weak call looks like a weak opener and you change the wrong thing. Judge an opener on the stage it actually affects, which is conversation rate.

### The diagnosis: five numbers, and the one that locates the problem (mode C)

Before rewriting a script, find out whether the script is what is broken. Usually it is not.

| Number | Needs work | Solid | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Connect rate, verified mobiles | Under 15% | 15 to 25% | Above 25% |
| Connect rate, mixed list | Under 8% | 8 to 12% | Above 12% |
| Conversation to meeting | Under 15% | 15 to 25% | Above 25% |
| Meetings per 100 dials | Under 1 | 1 to 2 | Above 2 |
| Meeting show rate | Under 70% | 70 to 85% | Above 85% |

Where to look when a number is in the needs-work band:

- **Connect rate low: a data problem.** Wrong numbers, landlines, bad timing. No opener fixes any of that, and rewriting the script here is the single most common wasted fortnight in outbound. Fix: verified mobile data and call timing. Verified mobiles alone move the mixed-list connect band from 8 to 12 percent up to 15 to 25 percent, which is also what gets an A/B test to a readable sample inside a fortnight.
- **Conversation rate low: a delivery problem, then the opener.** They picked up and left inside thirty seconds. Record yourself, slow down, drop the inflection first; this is the one number the nine scripts can move.
- **Show rate low: a booking problem.** They agreed and did not turn up, so the meeting was easier to accept than to attend. Confirm the reason, not just the time.
- **Meetings per 100 low while connect and conversation are fine: a downstream call problem**, not the opener at all.

Keep connect and conversation separate and you always know whether to go fix your phone data or go record yourself. Treat the bands as orientation, not targets; the only benchmark that counts is the user's own last month.

### Hard guardrails (state the relevant ones; never break them)

- **Never reference or use an intent signal.** Saying you saw them on your website or opening an email reads as surveillance and ends the call. Use intent to decide who to call, never to open.
- **Never claim to have looked at something you did not.** They will ask what you saw.
- **Never mention a trigger and stop.** A signal without the consequence it creates is flattery, not relevance; the bridge is mandatory.
- **Never apologise for calling**, in the words or the tone.
- **Never overstate a peer connection.** "Your name came up" has to be true or defensibly true.
- **Never cram the reason, the pitch and the ask into the first breath.**
- **Never rewrite the script when the connect rate is the weak number.** That is a data problem.
- **Never test nine openers at once, switch openers day to day on feel, or declare a winner early.**
- **Never judge an opener on meetings booked.** That measures the whole call.
- **Never run a generic problem in opener 3.** If they would think "that is everyone" rather than "that is me", it fails.

### The worksheet (set the test up before the first dial)

The two you are testing (one per tier), the one variable (the opener only, write down what you hold constant), the list (one segment, unchanged for two weeks), the reason for calling (same for both), the time of day (and alternate by call), connects counted A and B (target a hundred each), still-talking-at-thirty-seconds (the deciding number), and the start and end date (write the end date now). Before declaring a winner, ask one question: is the gap big and consistent enough that you would bet next quarter on it? If the honest answer is no, keep dialling; the test is not finished.

### Fourteen ways to lose the call in the first ten seconds (audit)

Opening with your name and company; claiming to have looked at something you did not; cramming everything into the first breath; referencing an intent signal; speaking too fast (the most common tell); mentioning a trigger and stopping; upward inflection on statements; overstating a peer connection; apologising for calling; switching openers every day on feel; a generic problem in opener 3; testing nine at once; judging on meetings booked; rewriting the script when the connect rate is low. Most sellers are doing three of these and have never heard themselves do any of them. The only way to find yours: record a week, listen to ten, tick honestly.

### Where to start (one opener, one list, one number)

Record ten calls before changing anything, so you know how you currently sound. Find your pattern (it is almost always pace). Pick one tier-one opener and learn it well enough to stop reading it. Slow down and drop the inflection, two changes only, because delivery moves the number faster than words. Add a tier-two opener and alternate by call. Count conversations, still talking at thirty seconds, and read the result honestly at a hundred connects each. Then change one thing again next fortnight, the reason for calling or the time of day, not the opener again. One variable at a time is slower for a fortnight and far faster over a year. Teaching the opener to one other person is the fastest way to find the part of it you do not yet understand.

### Worked examples (dummy data, every name and number invented)

**A tier-one opener filled (problem first), for a seller of onboarding software calling Heads of Sales at fifty-person SaaS firms:**

> "Hey Dana, quick one and then I'll get out of your way. Most Heads of Sales I speak to at fifty-person SaaS companies are watching new-rep ramp eat a full quarter right now. Is that landing anywhere near you, or are you in better shape than most?"
> Delivery: land the pause before the question, downward inflection on "than most", no lift at the end.

**A tier-three opener filled (signal led, with the bridge):**

> "Hi Dana, Sam from Northwind. I saw the four SDR roles you posted this month and it made me want to call rather than email. Teams hiring that fast usually run into managers coaching four new starters at once about a quarter later. Have you hit that yet, or has it not surfaced?"
> The bridge is line three; without it the signal is just flattery.

**A worked diagnosis:** connect rate 6 percent on a mixed list, conversation rate 18 percent, show rate 82 percent. The weak number is the connect rate, which is a data problem, not a script problem. Verdict: do not touch the opener. Get verified mobiles and fix the call window first; the conversation and show rates say the opener and the booking are already fine.
